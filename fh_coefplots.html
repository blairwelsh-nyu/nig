Farmer–Herder Conflict and Women's Access to Health Services:
Evidence from Nigeria's Middle Belt

Working Paper  ·  Nigeria DHS 2003–2018  ·  October 2026

Abstract

We estimate the effect of farmer–herder conflict on women's healthcare utilization and educational attainment in Nigeria's Middle Belt. Using a difference-in-differences design that exploits variation in post-2009 Armed Conflict Location and Event Data (ACLED) conflict intensity across Local Government Areas, we compare outcomes in high-conflict (top-tercile) and low-conflict LGAs before (2003, 2008) and after (2013, 2018) the escalation of interethnic violence. Women in high-conflict LGAs experienced significant declines in skilled birth attendance (−6.9 percentage points), any prenatal care visits (−5.3 pp), and modern contraceptive use (−5.4 pp), alongside a 2.0-year reduction in completed schooling. A four-wave event study—exploiting the 2003 DHS as a genuine pre-treatment wave—confirms parallel trends for all primary outcomes and documents effects escalating after 2013. A placebo test using pre-2009 conflict intensity yields uniformly null results, ruling out selection on pre-existing differences. Together, the findings suggest that interethnic conflict disrupts access to formal health and education services beyond the direct costs of violence, with implications for public health delivery in conflict-affected regions.
