Farmer–Herder Conflict and Women's Access to Health Services:
Evidence from Nigeria's Middle Belt

Working Paper  ·  Nigeria DHS 2003–2018  ·  October 2026

Abstract

We estimate the causal effect of farmer–herder conflict on women's healthcare utilization in Nigeria's Middle Belt. Using a four-wave difference-in-differences design (2003, 2008, 2013, 2018) that exploits variation in post-2009 ACLED conflict intensity across Local Government Areas, we find that women in high-conflict LGAs experienced significant declines in skilled birth attendance (−7.3 percentage points), prenatal care visits (−5.6 pp), and modern contraceptive use (−5.3 pp), alongside a 5.3 pp increase in unattended births. A four-wave event study using the 2003 DHS as a pre-treatment wave finds no evidence against parallel pre-trends for any outcome. Positive mechanism tests point to supply-side disruption: conflict increases women's self-reported financial access barriers (+8.3 pp, p = 0.002); both public and private facility births decline; wealthier women bear larger skilled-delivery reductions; and a dose-response gradient is significant across all outcomes. The Fulani–non-Fulani interaction is not significant for any outcome (p > 0.6), consistent with facility-level disruption rather than targeted exclusion, though within-group estimates are imprecise. The results are robust to leave-one-state-out jackknifes, Conley spatial standard errors, BH violence controls, and alternative treatment definitions, and survive a randomization inference test at p = 0.078.

Ethnicity & Religion Tests

The farmer–herder conflict pits predominantly Muslim Fulani pastoralists against predominantly Christian farming communities (Tiv, Berom, Tarok). If conflict causes targeted disruption or exclusion of one group, we should see differential treatment effects. Wave-specific ethnicity codes are used throughout (2008: Fulani=v131 code 2; 2013: code 109; 2018: code 2). N = 5,169 Fulani women (14.3% of the Middle Belt sample).

Instrumental Variables

Four geographic instruments—each interacted with a post-2009 indicator—are constructed as candidate instruments for LGA-level conflict intensity. Z1 (rainfall stability) uses the inverse coefficient of variation of annual CHIRPS rainfall (2005–2018); it is discarded because it exhibits a sign reversal in the second stage, consistent with weak-instrument behaviour even though it clears the conventional F threshold. Z2 (Boko Haram proximity) is the inverse distance to Maiduguri; it fails the first stage (F = 1.8) and is discarded. The two remaining instruments use long-run climate data from WorldClim v2.1. Z3 (agro-ecological transition zone) scores LGAs by their proximity to the forest–savanna transition belt (~1,200–1,800 mm annual precipitation), where farmer–herder competition is structurally elevated. Z4 (tsetse/cattle suitability) uses temperature and precipitation jointly to proxy historical tsetse-fly suitability; tsetse historically excluded cattle-keeping from humid zones, so high-suitability LGAs have less tradition of coexistence and more conflict when Fulani herders move in post-2009. Both instruments are predetermined by long-run climate; their level effects on health are absorbed by LGA fixed effects.

Exclusion restriction caveat. The main threat to validity is that ecological zone predicts health outcomes through pathways other than farmer–herder conflict. High-precipitation transition zones have higher malaria burden, denser vegetation (affecting infrastructure), and were more likely to receive certain health programme expansions (e.g., urban-edge facility investment). Agricultural productivity shocks correlated with the transition zone could independently affect utilisation. The instruments are used primarily to verify the sign of the OLS effect and check for attenuation bias; they do not fully resolve confounding from these ecological channels. The OLS DiD remains the preferred estimate.

Z4 mechanism: The tsetse fly historically prevented cattle-keeping in humid zones (Rogers & Randolph 1993). High tsetse suitability (warm, humid) identifies historically farming-only LGAs with limited pastoral tradition—where Fulani herder in-migration post-2009 produces greater conflict than in areas accustomed to mixed land use. Instrument: Gaussian temperature suitability × sigmoid precipitation suitability, standardised at LGA level. Residual exclusion-restriction concern: humid LGAs also have higher malaria endemicity, which may independently reduce facility-based delivery through fever-related barriers and caregiver illness.

2SLS estimates (Figure 4 below): Both Z3 and Z4 produce estimates that are consistent in sign with OLS and significant at p < 0.001 for all four outcomes. However, the magnitudes are implausibly large: Z3 implies 5–6× amplification over OLS (skilled delivery −0.216 vs. −0.036 OLS); Z4 implies 9–10× (−0.333). At these scales, the Z4 estimate alone would reduce skilled delivery from a 50% baseline to approximately 17%, which is at the outer bound of plausibility even in severely conflict-affected areas. Likely explanations include: (i) LATE amplification—compliers are the most conflict-exposed LGAs, where effects may genuinely be larger; (ii) attenuation bias in OLS from measurement error in LGA-level conflict counts; (iii) incomplete exclusion restriction—the ecological instruments may capture correlated health pathway variation. Note: Z3 and Z4 are moderately correlated (r = −0.45) and share overlapping ecological mechanisms; they are not independent. The correct critical value for weak-instrument tests with cluster-robust SEs is the Montiel Olea–Pflueger effective F ≈ 23.1 (10% size distortion); Z3 passes this, Z4 is borderline. The convergence in sign across both instruments and OLS supports the direction of the effect; the 2SLS magnitudes should be treated as upper bounds rather than point estimates.
