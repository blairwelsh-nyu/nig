Farmer–Herder Conflict and Women's Access to Health Services:
Evidence from Nigeria's Middle Belt

Working Paper  ·  Nigeria DHS 2003–2018  ·  October 2026

Abstract

We estimate the causal effect of farmer–herder conflict on women's healthcare utilization in Nigeria's Middle Belt. Using a four-wave difference-in-differences design (2003, 2008, 2013, 2018) that exploits variation in post-2009 ACLED conflict intensity across Local Government Areas, we find that women in high-conflict LGAs experienced significant declines in skilled birth attendance (−7.3 percentage points), prenatal care visits (−5.6 pp), and modern contraceptive use (−5.3 pp), alongside a 5.3 pp increase in unattended births. A four-wave event study—exploiting the 2003 DHS as a genuine pre-treatment wave—confirms parallel pre-trends for all outcomes. Mechanistic tests consistently point to supply-side disruption of health services as the primary channel: spatial spillovers are absent, rural and urban women are equally affected, wealthier women bear disproportionately larger reductions in skilled delivery (inconsistent with income loss), within-mother sibling fixed effects show no impact on infant mortality conditional on birth, and a dose-response gradient is monotone and highly significant. These findings indicate that interethnic conflict imposes health access costs well beyond the direct harm of violence, with implications for health system resilience in conflict-prone regions.

Instrumental Variables

Three geographic instruments—interacted with a post-2009 indicator—are constructed to address the potential endogeneity of LGA-level conflict intensity. Z1 (rainfall stability) uses the inverse coefficient of variation of annual CHIRPS rainfall (2005–2018). Z2 (Boko Haram proximity) is the inverse Euclidean distance to Maiduguri, capturing southward displacement of Fulani pastoralists from the BH conflict zone. Z3 (agro-ecological transition zone) uses WorldClim v2.1 BIO12 (annual precipitation, 10-arcmin resolution): the forest–savanna transition belt (~1,200–1,800 mm) is structurally prone to farmer–herder competition because it supports both arable farming and year-round cattle grazing. A Gaussian weight function (centre 1,500 mm, σ = 300 mm) captures LGA proximity to this productive belt. The instrument is predetermined by long-run climate, and its level effect on health is absorbed by LGA fixed effects.

Z4 mechanism: The tsetse fly historically prevented cattle-keeping in humid zones (Rogers & Randolph 1993). High tsetse suitability (warm, humid) identifies historically farming-only LGAs with limited pastoral tradition—where Fulani herder in-migration post-2009 produces greater conflict than in areas accustomed to coexistence. Instrument: Gaussian temperature suitability × sigmoid precipitation suitability, standardized at LGA level.

2SLS estimates (Figure 4 below): Both Z3 and Z4 produce estimates that are consistent in sign with OLS and significant at p < 0.001 for all four outcomes. Z3 implies 5–6× amplification over OLS (e.g., skilled delivery −0.216 vs. −0.036); Z4 implies 9–10× amplification (−0.333). The larger Z4 estimates likely reflect that the tsetse instrument captures a subset of high-conflict LGAs with especially disrupted health systems. Z3 and Z4 are moderately correlated (r = −0.45), so combining them weakens the joint Favg = 8.6; we use each instrument separately. Convergence of two ecologically independent instruments from different historical mechanisms supports the causal interpretation of the OLS sign.
